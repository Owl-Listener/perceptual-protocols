# Reading

A short, opinionated list. The work this family of protocols builds on, and the work it sits alongside.

This is not a complete bibliography. It is the list I would hand a designer who wanted to do this work seriously — academically grounded enough to defend, practical enough to use.

Ten entries grouped into four themes, plus a handful of honourable mentions at the bottom.

---

## The foundations of aesthetic interaction

### 1. Jeffrey Bardzell, 2011, Interacting with Computers. **Interaction Criticism: An Introduction to the Practice.**

The founding text for treating interfaces as objects of aesthetic critique, not just usability artefacts. Bardzell argues that interaction design has matured enough that practitioners need to be able to talk about the quality of an interaction the way film critics talk about cinema. With vocabulary, with traditions, with informed disagreement.

The reason it matters here is that perceptual-protocols is, in effect, a tooling answer to Bardzell's question. If we accept that interfaces have an aesthetics, what do we do with that fact when an AI agent is now part of the team making the work? The vocabulary of qualities, the named tensions, the references-as-anchors — those are interaction criticism, exported into a format an agent can read.

If you only read one paper on this list, read this one.

[Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article/23/6/604/758707)

---

### 2. Donald A. Norman, 2004, Basic Books. **Emotional Design: Why We Love (or Hate) Everyday Things.**

The book that gave practitioners three useful words for talking about feeling in design: visceral (immediate, body-level response), behavioural (use-time experience), and reflective (what owning and using the thing means to me afterward).

What matters for perceptual-protocols is the visceral and reflective layers. AI agents can already do most of the behavioural work — they know how a button works. What they can't yet do is reliably produce the visceral first impression or the reflective aftertaste, because those layers depend on aesthetic intent that nobody has yet handed them in a structured way. mood-protocol and vocab-protocol are early attempts to make those layers explicit.

Cite this when you need to explain the perceptual gap to someone who hasn't yet felt it.

## Empirical research on how designers actually work

### 3. Andrés Lucero, Lena Hegemann & Antti Oulasvirta, 2019, CHI '19. **The Role of Mood Boards in Designerly Practice.**

Empirical work on how designers actually use mood boards in practice, across architecture, industrial design, fashion, and digital product design. The paper goes beyond the obvious "mood boards are useful" into the specific moves designers make: how they curate references, how they group them, how they argue with collaborators using them, how the board changes as the project matures.

This is the citation that makes the whole mood-protocol position defensible. Mood boards are not an informal tool — they are a high-bandwidth, structured, professional communication device. We just never built one for agents.

Read it before you write anything serious about why designers' visual practice deserves a format agents can read.

[Lucero's research page](https://users.aalto.fi/~luceroa1/research/moodboard+ai/)

---

### 4. UC Berkeley School of Information, 2026. **Aesthetic Taste and Its Limits: Breakdowns in Prompt-Mediated Design of User Interfaces.**

The most current academic work on the exact problem this family is trying to solve. How aesthetic intent breaks down when designers try to communicate it through prompts to AI. The researchers studied where designers got tripped up — the gaps between what they meant, what they said, and what the model produced.

The findings map almost directly onto the vocab-protocol design rationale. Aesthetic terms collapse under loose use. Designers don't share a vocabulary even within a single team. The same word means different things to different practitioners. The protocol's response is to anchor every term with references and anti-references, making the vocabulary self-defining at the point of use.

[Berkeley iSchool project page](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/projects/2026/aesthetic-taste-and-its-limits-breakdowns-prompt-mediated-design-user-interfaces)

## Aesthetic philosophy

### 5. Sianne Ngai, 2012, Harvard University Press. **Our Aesthetic Categories: Zany, Cute, Interesting.**

A philosophy book that names three aesthetic experiences which structure modern life: the zany, the cute, and the interesting. None of them are beautiful, and that is the point. Ngai argues that the aesthetic categories that matter today are not the ones inherited from 18th-century philosophy. The new ones emerged with mass production, mediated culture, and information work.

What matters most here is the category of the interesting. Ngai describes it as the aesthetic of information — the experience of wanting to keep looking without quite knowing why. It is the aesthetic that AI-generated content is best at producing, and arguably the aesthetic most designers are trying to escape. Naming it lets you fight it. It also makes clear that we will need new aesthetic vocabulary for the next decade. The old categories aren't enough.

---

### 6. Richard Shusterman, in **The Encyclopedia of Human-Computer Interaction, 2nd Ed.** Somaesthetics.

A philosophy of bodily aesthetic experience. Shusterman argues that aesthetics is not just about contemplation. It is about the body, the senses, the moment-by-moment experience of perceiving and acting. Applied to interaction design, this means the aesthetic of a product is not its visual style. It is the full sensory-motor experience of using it.

This is the reference for anything in the perceptual-protocols family that deals with materiality, friction, rhythm, or any quality that lives in the body of the user rather than the eye of the observer. Shusterman gives the academic grounding for an otherwise easily dismissed argument: interfaces feel like something, and the felt quality matters.

[IxDF Encyclopedia](https://ixdf.org/literature/book/the-encyclopedia-of-human-computer-interaction-2nd-ed/somaesthetics)

### 7. John Berger, 1972, BBC and Penguin. **Ways of Seeing.**

Not an HCI text. The text. Berger argues that looking is never neutral. Every act of perception is conditioned by what we have been taught to look for, by the technologies of reproduction that mediate what we see, and by the structures of power that decide whose seeing matters. Originally a BBC television series, later a small book, still on every serious designer's shelf.

The reason it belongs on this list is that perceptual-protocols is, at its heart, an argument that AI's aesthetic blindness is not an absence. It is a particular way of seeing. The model has been trained to see, just not on the things designers see for. Berger gives the language for naming that, and for taking the project of teaching machines to see what we see seriously, as a cultural project rather than a technical one.

If you have not read this since design school, re-read it.

## Affect, emotion, and machines

### 8. Rosalind W. Picard, 1997, MIT Press. **Affective Computing.**

The book that named the field. Picard argued, at a time when this was contentious, that machines would need to perceive, model, and respond to human emotion in order to be useful collaborators. Most of what has been built since — sentiment analysis, mood-aware adaptive interfaces, emotion-recognition systems — descends from this argument.

The reason it matters for perceptual-protocols is that Picard's thesis was about machine perception of human feeling. The protocols here invert it. They are about human transmission of feeling to the machine. Both directions are necessary. The combination is what genuine collaboration looks like.

---

### 9. Lars Hallnäs & Johan Redström, 2001, Personal and Ubiquitous Computing. **Slow Technology — Designing for Reflection.**

A short, sharp paper that proposes slow technology as a counter-direction to the efficiency-and-speed default of HCI. The argument: not every interaction wants to be fast. Some interactions are improved by deliberate slowness, by friction, by waiting, by being asked to attend.

The connection to perceptual-protocols is direct. The canonical vocabulary names friction as a positive quality, and Hallnäs & Redström are the citation that earns that move. Use this when someone argues that adding friction is always bad design. It isn't.

---

### 10. Marc Hassenzahl, 2003 onward. **The Thing and I: Understanding the Relationship Between User and Product**, and related work on hedonic and pragmatic UX.

The cleanest two-axis model for talking about user experience. Pragmatic qualities are about what a product helps you do — efficient, clear, useful. Hedonic qualities are about what a product helps you be or feel — stimulating, beautiful, evocative. Most products optimise pragmatic and treat hedonic as decoration. Hassenzahl's argument is that this is wrong. Hedonic qualities are independently valuable and need their own design vocabulary.

Use this when you need to make the perceptual-design argument legible to a product manager. The pragmatic and hedonic split maps almost directly onto the procedural and perceptual split this family of protocols organises itself around.

## Honourable mentions

Brief notes on a few more worth knowing.

- **Jeffrey & Shaowen Bardzell, 2015, Humanistic HCI.** The longer arc of the work that Interaction Criticism opens. Worth reading if the connection between aesthetics, ethics, and HCI design practice interests you.
- **DesignPrompt** — recent multimodal-prompting research on how designers express intent to AI. A companion empirical study to Berkeley's Aesthetic Taste and Its Limits.
- **Stanford ArtEmis.** An AI system trained to identify emotional intent in classic artwork. The inverse of vocab-protocol's project — teach the machine to recognise feeling in existing work, rather than capturing it from a human in advance.
- **Pieter Desmet, multiple papers on emotional design and product attachment.** Empirical work on how feelings attach to objects. The methodological complement to Norman.
- **Aaron Walter, 2011, Designing for Emotion.** A practitioner book, more accessible than the academic literature. A good starting point if the others above feel intimidating.

## Contributing to this list

This list is a working document. The criteria for adding an entry are simple: does the work change how someone might design or build in this space, and is the annotation useful enough that a reader can decide whether to follow up?

Pull requests welcome. The bar for a new entry is one paragraph of opinionated annotation. If you cannot argue why it matters, it is not ready to be on the list.
